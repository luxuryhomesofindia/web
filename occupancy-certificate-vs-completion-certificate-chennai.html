Executive Summary — Key Takeaways

A Completion Certificate (CC) verifies that construction matches the sanctioned building plan under Rule 20 of Tamil Nadu's Combined Development and Building Rules, 2019 (TNCDBR-2019). An Occupancy Certificate (OC) grants statutory permission to occupy the building. In Chennai, small residential buildings may qualify for specified CC exemptions, but an exemption from CC does not authorize unauthorized plan deviations or floor additions. Neither property tax receipts nor builder handover letters replace official statutory certificates.

A Completion Certificate (CC) and an Occupancy Certificate (OC) are related to building compliance, but they should not automatically be treated as interchangeable documents. A Completion Certificate generally concerns whether construction has been completed in accordance with the approved plan and applicable requirements. An Occupancy Certificate generally concerns permission to occupy a building under the relevant approval process. In Chennai, homeowners must first establish which procedure applies to their particular property, because terminology, requirements and exemptions depend on the governing rules and building category. Tamil Nadu's Combined Development and Building Rules, 2019, address Completion Certificates, but subsequent amendments must also be considered. Before accepting possession or occupying a newly built home, check the original planning permission, building permit, applicable completion documentation and as-built condition. Where the requirement is unclear, obtain confirmation from the competent authority and the registered professional responsible for the building approval.

1. What is a Completion Certificate?

A Completion Certificate generally records that a building has been completed in accordance with its approved plan and the requirements applicable to its approval.

In Tamil Nadu, Rule 20 of the Combined Development and Building Rules, 2019, addresses the Completion Certificate procedure. It describes the submission of completion documentation and identifies categories to which the requirement applies, alongside specified exemptions. The current applicable provisions must be checked against subsequent amendments before determining whether a particular building is exempt.

For a homeowner, the important questions are:

  • Does the building fall within a category for which completion documentation is required?
  • Has the completed construction been checked against the sanctioned plan?
  • Have the prescribed forms, professional certificates and other applicable documents been submitted?
  • Has the competent authority completed the required process?

A Completion Certificate should not be assumed to legalise unauthorised construction or resolve every planning, title or statutory issue.

2. What is an Occupancy Certificate?

An Occupancy Certificate generally relates to whether a building may be occupied under the applicable approval process.

The term is widely used in property transactions and apartment handovers. However, the precise terminology and process vary by jurisdiction and building category.

For a Chennai property, do not assume that a process described as an OC elsewhere applies in exactly the same way under Tamil Nadu's rules. Check the terminology and documentation specified by the authority responsible for the building's approval.

An occupancy-related approval is also different from a property-tax assessment, an electricity or water connection, or a builder's possession letter. These documents serve different purposes and should not be treated as substitutes for statutory approvals where those approvals are required.

4. Which Certificate Is Required for a New House in Chennai?

There is no safe universal answer for every independent house, apartment or residential development.

The applicable requirement depends on the building's characteristics, the approval route and the current rules. Rule 20 of Tamil Nadu's Combined Development and Building Rules, 2019, provides a Completion Certificate procedure and specifies exemptions. The original provisions have been amended over time, so an older online article may not reflect the current position.

Before deciding which documentation is required, verify:

  • The authority that issued the planning permission and building permit.
  • The building's use, height, dwelling-unit count and relevant measurements.
  • The precise exemption wording, if an exemption is being claimed.
  • Whether any later amendment or special rule affects the building.
  • Whether the competent authority requires a Completion Certificate or another prescribed process.
  • Whether the completed building conforms to the approved plan.

7. What If the Completed House Differs from the Sanctioned Plan?

Treat a deviation as a compliance issue to investigate, not simply as a missing-document problem.

Evaluating potential structural or setback plan deviations using scale models and approved drawings.

Potentially material differences include:

  • An additional unapproved floor or terrace room.
  • Changes to required setbacks or open spaces.
  • Increased built-up area exceeding permissible FSI.
  • A change to the approved building use (e.g., residential to commercial).

Ask a registered architect or engineer to compare the sanctioned drawings with the actual building and explain the appropriate procedure. Do not assume that paying a fee will automatically resolve an unauthorised deviation.

8. Can You Occupy a House Without a Completion Certificate or Occupancy Certificate?

Do not assume that physical completion alone authorises occupation. If completion or occupancy approval is required for your building category, confirm that the prescribed process has been completed before occupying the building.

9. Why These Documents Matter When Buying or Building a Home

  • Before buying a completed house: Request sanctioned plans, building permits, and completion certificates.
  • Before accepting an apartment: Request development approvals and CC covering both your unit and common areas.
  • Before releasing final construction payment: Specify in your contract that obtaining statutory completion documentation is the contractor's responsibility.
  • Before arranging utility connections: EB and Metro Water may require completion documentation depending on the building category.

10. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming CC and OC are identical everywhere.
  • Relying on outdated rules or unverified online threshold claims.
  • Assuming every independent house is exempt without checking current circulars.
  • Ignoring unauthorized setback deviations or extra floors.
  • Relying solely on a builder's possession or handover letter.
  • Treating property-tax receipts as proof of building approval.
