What to Include in a Builder Contract Before Starting House Construction

Published: October 2026 Luxury Homes of India Editorial 14 Min Read Chennai & Tamil Nadu
Contract Governance
Updated October 2026
14 Min Read

Executive Summary: Before starting house construction, a builder contract should clearly define the construction scope, materials and specifications, total price, payment milestones, completion timeline, quality inspections, change-order procedure, defect rectification, and final handover requirements. It should also identify the parties, property, approved drawings, applicable permissions, insurance responsibilities, and dispute-resolution process. For homeowners building an independent house or luxury villa in India, these terms help establish what the builder must deliver, what the homeowner must provide, how additional costs are approved, and how delays or defects are handled. Attach the agreed BOQ, drawings, specifications and schedules to the signed agreement, and ensure that conflicting documents are addressed explicitly. Before signing or paying a substantial advance, have the technical scope reviewed by a qualified construction professional and the contractual terms reviewed by a qualified lawyer.

Why a Detailed Builder Contract Matters

Building an independent house or luxury villa is a significant financial commitment. Before construction begins, a clear written agreement between the homeowner and builder helps define responsibilities, document expectations and reduce misunderstandings throughout the project.

A builder contract should do more than record the total construction price. It should explain what will be built, which materials will be used, what the quoted amount includes, when payments become due, how changes are approved and what happens if delays or defects occur.

For homeowners planning construction in Chennai or Chengalpattu, the agreement should also allocate responsibility for site conditions, applicable approvals, drainage, waterproofing, and coordination between civil, electrical, plumbing and interior works where these are included in the scope. Project-specific requirements should be confirmed by the relevant professionals rather than assumed to apply uniformly to every property.

This guide explains 18 essential clauses to review before signing a house construction agreement.

1. Complete Details of the Homeowner and Builder

Start by identifying every party entering into the agreement. Incomplete details can create confusion when payments, notices or contractual responsibilities need to be addressed.

Include:

  • Parties: Full legal names and addresses of the homeowner and builder.
  • Entity verification: The builder's registered business name and relevant registration details, where applicable.
  • Communication channels: Contact details for official communication and legal notices.
  • Authority: The authorised signatory's name and designation, if a company is executing the agreement.
  • Jurisdiction & date: The date and place of execution.
  • Site coordinates: The proposed construction site's complete address.

Homeowner Due Diligence Check: If there are multiple property owners, confirm whether all relevant owners need to sign. Have your legal reviewer verify the correct parties and corporate signing authority before execution.

2. Property Details and Construction Scope

The agreement must identify the property and define the work the builder is responsible for completing.

Include the plot number, survey number where relevant, location, plot area, proposed building configuration, and the drawings and schedules that define the project.

The scope should specify whether the builder is responsible for:

  • Civil groundwork: Site preparation, excavation and foundation.
  • Superstructure: Structural work, masonry and plastering.
  • Protection: Roofing, waterproofing and drainage.
  • MEP infrastructure: Electrical and plumbing installations.
  • Architectural finishes: Flooring, doors, windows and painting.
  • Interior fitments: Kitchen, wardrobes and other interior works.
  • External civil works: External paving, compound walls and landscaping.
  • Handover protocols: Testing, cleaning and final handover.

Clearly identify excluded work. A quotation may exclude borewell drilling, utility connection charges, compound walls, loose furniture, certain appliances or other external works.

Attach the agreed architectural drawings, structural drawings, specifications and detailed scope of work. State how conflicts between these documents will be resolved.

3. Detailed Material Specifications

A promise to use “good-quality materials” is not sufficiently precise for a complex construction project. Specify the required materials, grades, dimensions, brands where relevant, technical standards, finishes and approved alternatives.

Work Category What to Define in the Contract
Cement and concrete Concrete grades (e.g., M20, M25), design or mix requirements, ready-mix suppliers, and applicable BIS curing specifications.
Reinforcement steel Required grade (Fe 500D / Fe 550D), primary mill brands, applicable IS 1786 standards and batch test certificate requirements.
Masonry Clay brick or AAC block type, compressive strength, mortar ratios, dimensions and performance requirements.
Waterproofing System chemistry (elastomeric, crystalline, bituminous membrane), application areas, surface preparation and pond-test testing procedures.
Flooring Material type (Italian marble, vitrified tiles, hardwood), slab thickness, dimensions, finish, laying mortar, and approved brand or equivalent.
Doors and windows Frame material (teak, UPVC, thermal-break aluminium), profile gauges, architectural hardware brands, locksets, and double-glazed unit (DGU) glass specifications.
Electrical Fire-retardant (FRLS) copper wiring brands, modular switch ranges, distribution boards, MCB/RCCB protection devices and point allocations.
Plumbing Pipe materials (CPVC, UPVC, SWR), pressure ratings (Class 1, SDR 11), concealed fittings, valves, and sanitaryware brand/model ranges.
Painting Surface preparation, primer coats, acrylic putty layers, and exterior weather-proof or interior luxury emulsion coating systems.
Fixtures Brand, model numbers or agreed provisional quality allowances per unit, where applicable.

The contract should explain how substitutions are approved and whether a substitution changes the price or schedule.

4. Total Construction Cost and Price Inclusions

State the agreed construction price and explain how it is calculated. Identify whether the arrangement is a lump-sum contract, a rate-per-square-foot contract, a cost-plus arrangement or another pricing model.

The agreement should clarify:

  • Measurement basis: The exact measurement basis and chargeable area formula (carpet area vs. built-up area vs. super built-up area).
  • Resource scope: Whether labour, materials, equipment, shuttering, scaffolding and on-site supervision are included.
  • Statutory tax: Applicable GST and other statutory levies.
  • Design fees: Whether architectural, structural, electrical and plumbing design consultancy fees are included.
  • Permit charges: Whether CMDA/GCC approval-related liaison fees and consultant charges are included.
  • Allied services: Whether electrical, plumbing, interiors, hardscaping and external development are included.
  • Allowances: Any provisional sums, prime-cost allowances or explicitly excluded items.
  • Valuation mechanism: The specific method for valuing additional, altered or omitted work.

For a per-square-foot contract, define how the area is measured and how balconies, terraces, staircases, utility spaces and other areas are treated.

Where appropriate, attach a detailed bill of quantities (BOQ) or itemised cost schedule. Compare quotations on the same scope and measurement basis so that omitted work does not create an unexpected cost later.

5. Payment Schedule Linked to Construction Milestones

Payments should be documented and linked to agreed, verifiable stages rather than vague statements such as “payment as required.”

Possible milestones include foundation completion, structural work, masonry, services, finishing and handover. The actual milestones and percentages must be negotiated for the project; there is no universal schedule suitable for every house.

Illustrative Milestone What to Verify Before Releasing Funds
Mobilisation Agreement signed, architectural/structural drawings released, site barricading, temporary power/water arranged, and agreed mobilisation requirements fulfilled.
Foundation Excavation completed to firm soil stratum, anti-termite treatment applied, footing rebar inspected, concrete poured, and backfilling completed and inspected.
Structure Agreed structural milestone (columns, beams, and intermediate or roof slab) cast, de-shuttered, cured, and cube compressive strength tests verified.
Masonry and plastering Specified brickwork/blockwork completed, door frames fixed, internal and external plastering cured with straight vertical alignment checked.
Electrical & plumbing rough-in Wall chases cut, conduit boxes placed, water supply and drain lines pressure-tested (hydro-tested) to ensure zero leaks prior to plaster closure.
Finishing Contracted tile/marble flooring laid, wall putty applied, primary coats painted, sanitaryware and electrical switch plates mounted.
Handover Joint snagging inspection completed, agreed rectifications addressed, keys, warranties, and as-built drawings formally delivered.

Specify invoice requirements, payment due dates, applicable taxes, the process for disputing invoices and the consequences of delayed payment.

Avoid paying for work that has not reached the agreed milestone unless an advance or procurement payment is expressly provided for and supported by suitable contractual safeguards.

6. Construction Start Date and Completion Deadline

A useful construction timeline identifies when work is expected to start, what must happen before commencement and how completion will be assessed.

Include:

  • Planned commencement: The planned date of ground-breaking or site possession.
  • Pre-conditions: Conditions that must be satisfied before construction begins (approvals, site clearance, advance deposit).
  • Substantial completion: The target date for substantial structural and civil completion.
  • Handover target: The expected date or process for final handover and keys transfer.
  • Interim deadlines: Major intermediate milestones (e.g., plinth completion, roof slab casting).
  • Programme revisions: The procedure for updating and re-baselining the construction programme.
  • Evaluation: How delays and extensions of time will be assessed and documented.

Specify whether the timeline begins after site possession, drawing approval, statutory permission, mobilisation or another agreed event. Define completion clearly enough that it can be checked against the actual scope.

7. Delay, Extension of Time and Compensation

The agreement should explain what happens when either party causes a delay.

Distinguish between builder-caused delays, late payments, delayed design decisions, homeowner-requested changes, approval delays, exceptional weather and other events outside the parties' reasonable control.

Define:

  • Notification protocols: The mandatory written process and time limit for notifying the other party of a delay event.
  • Evidentiary logs: The evidence, daily site logs, weather reports, and records required to support a claim.
  • Time extensions: Whether an extension of time (EOT) is available and the exact formula by which it is calculated.
  • Liquidated damages: Any agreed compensation or liquidated damages (e.g., a defined rate per week of unexcused delay) for attributable builder delays.
  • Liability caps: Applicable financial limits, grace periods, and force majeure exceptions.

Contract Risk Alert: Unlimited Extensions

Avoid an open-ended extension clause that allows delays for broad, unverified reasons. Have a qualified construction contract lawyer review the proposed liquidated damages and EOT provisions to verify their legal enforceability.

8. Construction Quality Standards and Inspection Rights

The builder should agree to execute the work in accordance with the contract documents, applicable approvals, relevant technical requirements and specified workmanship standards.

Specify:

  • Structural hold-points: Mandatory inspection stages for foundation depth, shuttering alignment, and reinforcement steel binding prior to concrete placement.
  • Mill test certificates: Required batch test certificates for cement, TMT steel, and aggregates.
  • Laboratory testing: Concrete cube compressive strength testing at 7 and 28 days as per IS 516.
  • Hydraulic testing: Waterproofing ponding tests (minimum 72 hours) and pressurized plumbing line leakage tests.
  • Electrical safety: Megger insulation resistance testing, loop impedance, and earthing pit resistance checks.
  • Defect remediation: Formal protocols for logging non-conforming work in an on-site register and establishing rectification deadlines.
  • Cost allocation: Confirmation that the builder bears all remedial costs when work fails agreed technical specifications.

The agreement should allow reasonable site inspections by the homeowner or an appointed qualified professional, with appropriate safety and coordination arrangements.

Where independent inspections are required, define their scope, frequency, notice requirements, documentation and cost responsibility.

9. Approved Drawings and Statutory Permissions

The agreement should establish that construction will follow the applicable approved plans and agreed technical documents.

Clarify who is responsible for:

  • Architectural coordination: Preparing, modifying, and coordinating working architectural drawings.
  • Structural documentation: Structural calculations, soil compliance, and engineering drawings signed by a licensed structural engineer.
  • Permit submission: Submitting and coordinating building permit applications with local civic bodies (e.g., GCC, CMDA, DTCP).
  • Statutory liaison: Managing submissions, physical inspections, and fee receipts.
  • Authority fees: Clarifying whether statutory development charges, infrastructure amenities charges, and regularisation fees are borne by the owner or included in the package.
  • Revision management: Managing architectural revisions necessitated by planning authority queries.
  • Record keeping: Maintaining approved blue-prints, sanction permits, and inspection books securely on site.

Responsibilities depend on the project arrangement and local requirements. Assistance with an application is not a guarantee of approval. Confirm the applicable requirements for the specific property and authority before construction begins.

10. Variations, Upgrades and Additional Work

Changes during construction can affect costs, procurement and completion dates. The agreement should establish a written variation process before any additional or altered work proceeds.

Each change order should record:

  • Description: The requested change, the reason for the variation, and its precise physical location.
  • Drawings: The revised architectural or engineering drawing, if applicable.
  • Cost impact: The itemised additional cost, rate analysis, or credit adjustment.
  • Cashflow: Any modification to the milestone payment schedule.
  • Timeline: The quantifiable effect on the substantial completion date.
  • Sign-off: Formal written approval from both authorised signatories prior to executing the work.

For example, upgrading from standard flooring to premium natural stone may affect the material price, installation method, procurement time and finishing sequence. Record the agreed cost and schedule implications before authorising the change.

11. Site Supervision and Progress Reporting

The homeowner should know who is managing the site and how progress will be communicated.

Include the designated site engineer or project manager's responsibilities, reporting frequency, meeting arrangements and escalation contacts.

A useful report can cover:

  • Executed tasks: Work completed during the reporting period.
  • Lookahead schedule: Planned activities and milestones for the upcoming 2 to 4 weeks.
  • Supply chain tracking: Material deliveries, procurement lead times, and factory fabrication status.
  • Quality audits: Inspection test results and logs of any outstanding quality rectifications.
  • Risk management: Delays, weather challenges, site constraints, and corrective action plans.
  • Visual evidence: High-resolution photographs and verified milestone status updates.

For a large independent house or luxury villa, agree on how the builder, architect, structural engineer, interior designer and specialist contractors will coordinate decisions where those roles are part of the project.

12. Labour, Subcontractors and Site Safety

Clarify who is responsible for labour deployment, subcontractor coordination, site housekeeping and safety arrangements.

Address:

  • Labour management: Timely labour payments, skilled trade deployment, and site discipline.
  • Statutory welfare: Compliance with applicable labour laws, workmen's compensation, and statutory welfare requirements.
  • Safety gear: Mandatory personal protective equipment (helmets, harnesses, safety boots) and safe working scaffolds.
  • Secure warehousing: Safe, locked storage of materials, power tools, and valuable hardware.
  • Access control: Visitor protocols, worker ID badges, and unauthorized personnel restrictions.
  • Housekeeping: Systematic waste removal, debris carting, and daily site cleaning.
  • Third-party indemnity: Builder's financial and legal liability for damage caused to adjoining properties.
  • Insurance policies: Adequate contractor's all-risk (CAR) and workmen's compensation insurance coverage.

Define how incidents, unsafe conditions and damage to neighbouring properties will be reported and addressed.

13. Material Procurement, Storage and Wastage

Material management can affect both cost and construction quality. Define who purchases each material, who approves suppliers, where materials are stored and how deliveries are documented.

Include procedures for checking quantities and specifications on delivery, recording damaged or rejected materials, protecting products from weather or theft, managing surplus materials, approving substitutions and reconciling unused owner-funded materials.

If the homeowner purchases materials directly, clarify the builder's responsibility for receiving, handling, installation and reporting shortages or defects.

14. Warranty, Defect Rectification and Maintenance

The agreement should explain the builder's obligations after completion.

Specify the applicable defect reporting period (often 12 months from handover for general defects, and 5 to 10 years for structural stability and waterproofing), how defects must be notified, the expected response and rectification timelines, and who bears the cost of correcting defects attributable to the builder's work.

Differentiate between:

  • Workmanship defects: Substandard plastering, hollow tiles, leaking joints, or poor carpentry finishing.
  • Material defects: Defective batches of paint, cracked sanitaryware, or faulty glass units.
  • OEM warranties: Manufacturer warranties for elevators, heat pumps, air conditioning, and solar systems.
  • Owner misuse: Damage caused by improper operation, lack of maintenance or unauthorised post-handover modifications.
  • Natural wear: Normal environmental weathering and fair wear and tear.
  • Structural warranty: Structural settlement or integrity obligations under applicable statutory rules.

Do not treat a general warranty clause as a replacement for legal obligations that may apply to the specific project. Have the proposed wording reviewed professionally.

15. Utilities, Testing and Final Handover

Define what completion means. A building that appears finished may still have incomplete testing, outstanding defects, missing documents or unfinished external works.

A handover schedule can include:

  • Scope verification: Completion of contracted construction items and finish schedules.
  • MEP commissioning: Full functional testing of electrical distribution, lighting, and plumbing systems.
  • Water-tightness: Verified waterproofing and water-tightness certifications for terraces and wet areas.
  • Machinery operation: Operational testing of sump pumps, pressure boosters, water filtration, and solar water heaters.
  • Site clearance: Thorough cleaning, window glass scrubbing, and complete removal of all construction debris.
  • Snag close-out: Formal rectification and joint re-inspection of recorded defects.
  • Hardware delivery: Handover of master keys, access keycards, remote controls, and equipment manuals.
  • Warranties: Original product warranty certificates and equipment service contracts.
  • As-built drawings: As-built architectural, structural, electrical, and plumbing conduit route plans.
  • Civic documentation: Completion Certificate (CC) or Occupancy Certificate (OC) documentation where contractually agreed.
  • Final settlement: Final account reconciliation, retention money release schedules, and mutually signed handover certificates.

Identify outstanding items, their completion dates and whether agreed retention or final payment depends on their resolution.

16. Insurance, Damage and Responsibility for Loss

Construction involves risks to workers, materials, neighbouring property and the partially completed structure.

Identify the insurance arrangements appropriate to the project and specify who obtains and maintains each required policy.

Consider construction-related risks, third-party liability, worker-related statutory coverage and protection of owner-supplied materials, as applicable.

Define responsibility for damage caused by the builder, subcontractors, the homeowner's separate contractors or events outside the parties' control. Verify actual coverage, exclusions, limits and policy periods rather than relying on a general statement that the site is insured.

17. Suspension, Termination and Final Settlement

The agreement should describe when either party may suspend work or terminate the contract.

Possible triggers include material contractual breaches, persistent non-payment, prolonged unexplained abandonment, insolvency where relevant, or failure to rectify serious non-compliance after proper notice.

Include:

  • Notice & cure periods: Mandatory written notice and a reasonable cure period (typically 14 to 30 days) to remedy the breach.
  • Suspension conditions: Legitimate grounds and protocols under which work may be temporarily paused.
  • Site preservation: Procedures for securing the building, preserving materials, and maintaining safety during suspension.
  • Valuation of work: Independent assessment and valuation of work executed up to the date of termination.
  • Material custody: Treatment and reconciliation of materials stored on site or pre-paid by the homeowner.
  • Property handover: Prompt surrender of site possession, gate keys, drawings, and permits to the owner.
  • Financial reconciliation: Final accounting of advances disbursed, work completed, debits, credits, and damages.
  • Records transfer: Transfer of all shop drawings, permit files, and structural calculation records.
  • Legal forum: Process for resolving any disputed balances.

Termination and settlement clauses should be fair, proportionate and legally reviewed.

18. Dispute Resolution and Contract Documents

Even a well-prepared agreement may not prevent every disagreement. Specify how disputes will be handled and which documents form part of the contract.

Define the process for written notices, negotiation, mediation or other agreed dispute-resolution mechanisms, and the appropriate legal forum or arbitration procedure where validly agreed.

List the contract documents, which may include:

  • Signed agreement and all numbered schedules.
  • Detailed BOQ and itemised cost breakdown.
  • Approved architectural and structural working drawings.
  • Material specifications and workmanship standards schedule.
  • Master construction schedule and milestone programme.
  • Payment milestone schedule and billing terms.
  • Written and signed variation/change orders.
  • Quality-inspection protocols and handover snagging checklists.

Document Priority & Hierarchy Rule: Agree in writing on the order of precedence for signed amendments, the main agreement, schedules, specifications, BOQ and drawings. Do not leave it to an informal understanding if two documents conflict. The final hierarchy should be reviewed by a qualified lawyer and the relevant technical professional.

Each party should retain a complete signed copy and records of subsequent amendments.

Builder Contract Checklist: Before You Sign

Use this checklist to review the agreement before making a substantial payment or allowing construction to begin.

Pre-Execution Builder Contract Audit Checklist

  • Homeowner and builder legal details, addresses, and corporate signing authorities are complete.
  • Property survey numbers, exact site boundaries, and construction scope are clearly identified.
  • Approved architectural plans, structural engineering drawings, and detailed BOQ are annexed.
  • Explicit lists of included and excluded works (e.g., compound walls, EB connections) are agreed.
  • Total lump-sum or per-sq-ft price, GST liabilities, and provisional sums are transparently defined.
  • Payment milestones are strictly tied to verifiable on-site physical engineering stages.
  • Definite project start date, pre-conditions, and target completion deadlines are stated.
  • Delay notification processes, extension of time rules, and liquidated damages are established.
  • Quality inspection hold-points, lab cube tests, and waterproofing pond tests are scheduled.
  • Responsibilities for CMDA/GCC planning approvals and statutory fees are assigned.
  • A mandatory written change-order procedure with prior cost/timeline approval is included.
  • Named on-site engineering supervision and periodic progress reporting formats are agreed.
  • Labour statutory compliance, personal safety gear, site housekeeping, and CAR insurance are allocated.
  • Defect liability period (12+ months) and structural/waterproofing warranty terms are clarified.
  • Final handover prerequisites (as-built plans, warranties, keys, test certificates) are specified.
  • Contract suspension, termination grounds, site custody, and final settlement formulas are clear.
  • A strict document hierarchy rule resolves any discrepancies between drawings, BOQ, and clauses.
  • All referenced schedules, drawings, and annexures are physically initialled and attached.
  • The final legal agreement has been vetted by an independent real estate lawyer.

Common Mistakes to Avoid in a Builder Contract

Critical Contractual Pitfalls for Homeowners

1. Using a one-page agreement for a complex project: A short document may omit important details about specifications, exclusions, variations and handover.

2. Accepting vague material descriptions: Terms such as “premium quality” do not replace measurable specifications with exact grades, brands, and thicknesses.

3. Paying without checking progress: Advance payments should be governed by clear contractual terms and appropriate safeguards rather than calendar dates.

4. Ignoring excluded items: A deceptively low quotation may omit significant external works, utility connections, interior fittings or approval-related costs.

5. Allowing verbal changes: Always record price and timeline implications in writing through a signed change order before authorising variations.

6. Leaving completion undefined: Specify the inspections, rectifications, documents and functional checks required before final handover and release of retention money.

7. Signing without independent review: A construction professional can review the technical scope, while a qualified lawyer can review contractual and legal risks.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Is a written builder contract necessary for an independent house?

A written agreement is strongly advisable because it records the agreed scope, price, materials, schedule, payment terms and responsibilities. The appropriate form and any execution or registration requirements should be confirmed for the specific transaction.

2. What is the most important clause in a construction agreement?

There is no single clause that protects every project. Scope of work, detailed specifications, price and exclusions, payment schedule, completion terms, variation procedures and defect rectification provisions work together to reduce ambiguity.

3. Should the BOQ be attached to the builder contract?

Where a BOQ forms part of the agreed pricing arrangement, it should be attached or clearly incorporated into the contract. Define the measurement method, item descriptions, rates, quantities and procedures for handling variations or discrepancies.

4. Can the builder increase the construction price after signing?

That depends on the contract and the circumstances. A clear agreement should distinguish approved variations, provisional sums, agreed price-adjustment mechanisms and costs that remain the builder's responsibility. Check the contractual basis and supporting details before accepting an increase.

5. How should construction payments be structured?

Payments can be linked to agreed, verifiable construction milestones. The schedule should also address advance payments, material procurement, invoices, inspection or certification where applicable, disputed amounts and final settlement.

6. What happens if the builder does not complete the house on time?

The agreement should define delay notification, permitted extensions, responsibility for the delay and any applicable compensation or other remedies. The outcome depends on the contract, the evidence and applicable law.

7. Should I include a warranty clause?

Yes. Define the defect reporting process, applicable periods, rectification responsibilities and exclusions. Distinguish manufacturer warranties from the builder's own contractual and statutory obligations.

8. Is a builder contract the same as a sale agreement?

No. A construction agreement generally establishes obligations for carrying out construction, while a sale agreement concerns the sale of property or a unit. A transaction involving both land and construction may require more than one document, depending on its structure and applicable law.

9. Should a lawyer review a house construction agreement?

For a significant construction investment, independent legal review is prudent. A construction professional can also review the technical scope, specifications, BOQ and milestone schedule to check that the commercial and technical documents are consistent.

Conclusion

A well-prepared builder contract turns a homeowner's expectations into documented responsibilities. It establishes what the builder will deliver, how the work will be measured and paid for, how changes will be managed, and what must be completed before handover.

Before signing, review the scope, specifications, exclusions, cost schedule, payment milestones, timeline, quality checks and defect rectification provisions together. The objective is not simply to prepare a longer contract. It is to create a clear, workable agreement that both parties understand.
