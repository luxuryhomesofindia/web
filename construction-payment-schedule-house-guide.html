Construction Payment Schedule for a House: Stage-Wise Milestones Explained

Published: October 2026 Luxury Homes of India Editorial 12–14 Min Read Chennai & Tamil Nadu
Building a house involves more than estimating the total construction cost. Homeowners also need to decide when payments are due, what work must be completed before each payment and how progress will be verified. A well-defined construction payment schedule connects agreed payments to measurable construction milestones, documented material purchases and clear acceptance criteria. It can help homeowners manage cash flow, reduce disputes and understand what they are paying for at each stage. The right schedule depends on the contract, scope of work, BOQ, procurement responsibilities and construction sequence. A labour-only agreement may require a different payment structure from a turnkey contract that includes materials and finishing. This guide explains how to structure milestone payments, calculate stage-wise amounts, handle advances and retention, check work before releasing payments, and prepare a written agreement. The percentages shown are illustrative examples for discussion, not mandatory industry standards or LHI's approved commercial terms.

1. What Is a Construction Payment Schedule?

A construction payment schedule is a written agreement showing how the contract amount will be paid across specified stages, activities or milestones.

Instead of paying the entire construction price in advance or relying only on calendar dates, the homeowner and contractor agree on what must happen before each payment becomes due.

A useful schedule specifies five things:

  • Milestone: The construction activity or stage being paid for.
  • Completion criteria: The work and checks required to qualify for payment.
  • Amount due: A percentage or fixed amount tied to the contract.
  • Verification: Who checks the work and what evidence is recorded.
  • Payment deadline: When payment must be made after the agreed conditions are met.

For example, “payment after the roof slab milestone is completed and the agreed inspection records are provided” is more precise than “payment after two months.”

Why does it matter?

A well-defined schedule can help homeowners:

  • Track expenditure against construction progress.
  • Coordinate labour payments and material procurement.
  • Reduce misunderstandings over payment dates.
  • Identify incomplete work before the next stage begins.
  • Record changes to the contract value.
  • Establish clear final-payment and handover conditions.

The schedule should also be workable for the contractor. Once the agreed milestone has been achieved and the required documents supplied, payment should be processed within the contractual period.

2. Sample Stage-Wise Construction Payment Schedule

The table below is an illustrative residential construction payment schedule. It is not a mandatory industry standard, a market benchmark or an approved LHI payment policy.

No. Illustrative milestone Payment
1 Agreement, mobilisation and site setup 5%
2 Foundation and plinth completion 15%
3 Agreed RCC structural work completion 20%
4 Masonry and specified plastering completion 15%
5 Concealed services and specified waterproofing checks 10%
6 Flooring, doors and windows as specified 15%
7 Painting, fixtures and agreed finishing work 10%
8 Final inspection and handover requirements 5%
9 Retention release after the agreed conditions are met 5%
Total 100%

Each milestone must be defined in the actual contract. Structural work may need to be divided into several stages, while other contracts may combine or separate activities differently.

The final 5% in this example represents retention released under agreed conditions. It is not necessarily payable on the handover date.

Before adopting any percentages, compare them with the BOQ, contract value, procurement arrangements, material ownership and the actual construction programme. A schedule should reflect the value of the work and materials it covers rather than simply distribute payments evenly across time.

What should be agreed before signing?

  • Whether the milestone is based on physical completion, inspection, certification or a combination.
  • Which drawings, specifications and BOQ items define the work.
  • How material advances and previously paid amounts are accounted for.
  • What happens if a milestone is only partially complete.
  • How variations, deductions and disputed amounts are handled.

3. What to Check Before Releasing Each Payment

A payment request should be checked against the agreed milestone, not accepted solely on the basis of a verbal update.

Milestone 1: Agreement and mobilisation

Confirm the signed agreement, identified drawings and specifications, scope of work, payment recipient and mobilisation conditions. Document any advance and how it will be adjusted against future payments.

Milestone 2: Foundation and plinth

Compare the completed scope with the approved drawings and relevant engineering requirements. Depending on the contract, this may include excavation, foundation work, reinforcement, concrete, plinth beams and associated activities.

Complete required inspections before the relevant work is concealed.

Milestone 3: RCC structural work

Identify precisely which columns, beams, slabs or other structural elements are covered by the payment. Check the agreed inspection and documentation requirements with the appropriately qualified professional.

Do not allow payment deadlines to override specified inspections, curing requirements or safety procedures.

Milestone 4: Masonry and plastering

Check the specified wall construction, openings, dimensions and plastering scope. Record incomplete items and agreed corrections.

Milestone 5: Concealed services and waterproofing

Check plumbing routes, electrical conduits and other concealed installations against the relevant drawings and specifications. Complete required tests and inspections before covering the work.

Use the specified waterproofing inspection and testing procedure where applicable.

Milestone 6: Flooring, doors and windows

Verify the agreed products, quantities, installation and acceptance criteria. Check operation, alignment, finish and outstanding work.

Milestone 7: Painting and finishing

Review the specified paint finish, fixtures, fittings and other included items. Prepare a documented list of incomplete or defective work.

Milestone 8: Final completion and handover

Inspect the completed contractual scope, test applicable services and collect the agreed handover documents, manuals, warranties and completion records.

Record outstanding items and establish a rectification process.

Milestone 9: Retention release

Release retention when the contractually specified conditions are satisfied. These may include completion of an agreed defect list, correction of specified defects and delivery of outstanding documents.

The release deadline and relationship to any post-handover obligations should be written into the contract.

4. Example: Calculate Construction Payments

Consider a hypothetical contract worth ₹40 lakh. If the parties agree to the illustrative percentages above, the calculations would be:

Milestone Percentage Amount
Agreement and mobilisation 5% ₹2,00,000
Foundation and plinth 15% ₹6,00,000
RCC structural work 20% ₹8,00,000
Masonry and plastering 15% ₹6,00,000
Concealed services and waterproofing 10% ₹4,00,000
Flooring, doors and windows 15% ₹6,00,000
Painting and finishing 10% ₹4,00,000
Final inspection and handover 5% ₹2,00,000
Retention release 5% ₹2,00,000
Total 100% ₹40,00,000
Calculation: Milestone amount = agreed contract value × milestone percentage

For example, 15% of ₹40,00,000 is ₹6,00,000.

These figures illustrate the arithmetic only. They are not a construction quotation, a prediction of actual project costs or a recommended universal payment allocation. Taxes, exclusions, approved variations and other contractual adjustments must be handled according to the actual agreement.

Before making a payment

  • Identify the milestone and its completion criteria.
  • Verify the work and review the required records.
  • Check approved variations and contractual adjustments.
  • Calculate the amount due.
  • Obtain the agreed invoice, certification or supporting documents.
  • Record the payment and update the cumulative total.

5. Advance Payments vs Milestone Payments

Advance payments and milestone payments serve different purposes.

Factor Advance payment Milestone payment
Timing Before associated work is completed When agreed conditions are met
Purpose May fund mobilisation or specified procurement Pays for an agreed stage of work
Main risk Money may be paid ahead of verifiable progress Vague completion criteria may cause disputes
Safeguard Document purpose, adjustment and appropriate protections Document inspection, evidence and payment deadline

How much advance should you pay?

There is no single advance percentage suitable for every residential construction agreement. The appropriate arrangement depends on the contract, procurement responsibilities, project scope and safeguards available.

Before paying an advance, establish:

  • What expenditure the advance is intended to cover.
  • How and when it will be adjusted.
  • Whether the materials will be delivered to the site or stored elsewhere.
  • Who owns materials purchased using the advance.
  • How unused funds and paid-for materials will be accounted for if work stops.
  • What contractual protection is appropriate for the arrangement.

Avoid large unsecured advances without understanding their purpose, accounting and contractual consequences.

6. What Is Retention Money in Construction?

Retention is an agreed portion of the contract price withheld until specified conditions are satisfied.

In residential construction, those conditions may include completing outstanding work, correcting agreed defects or providing the required handover documents.

Retention is a contractual arrangement, not a universal legal requirement for every private residential project.

A clear retention clause should specify:

  • The amount or percentage withheld.
  • Which payments it applies to.
  • The conditions for release.
  • The release deadline.
  • The process for recording and resolving defects.
  • Any relationship to the agreed defect-liability period.
  • How disputed amounts will be handled.

Retention should be proportionate to the agreement and should not be used to withhold unrelated amounts without a contractual basis.

7. Labour-Only vs Turnkey Construction Payment Schedules

The contract type affects how payments and purchasing responsibilities should be organised.

Factor Labour-only contract Turnkey or material-inclusive contract
Material procurement Generally arranged by the owner Generally handled by the contractor for included items
Payment basis Agreed labour scope and progress Contracted scope, potentially including labour and materials
Supplier payments Often managed separately by the owner May be included in contractor payments
Verification Check completed work and labour scope Check work and material/specification compliance
Budget control Track supplier spending and labour payments together Verify inclusions, procurement, milestone values and variations

For a labour-only contract, the owner should separately track material orders, deliveries, supplier payments and remaining labour commitments.

For a turnkey agreement, confirm the included materials, specifications, allowances, exclusions and procurement responsibilities before approving the schedule.

Do not assume that every turnkey agreement includes the same materials or finishing items. The signed scope and BOQ determine what is included.

8. Common Construction Payment Mistakes to Avoid

  • Paying too far ahead of progress: Check cumulative payments against the agreed value of completed work and any properly accounted-for materials.
  • Using vague milestones: Define the work, completion criteria, inspection evidence and payment deadline.
  • Paying only by calendar date: Where appropriate, use measurable work or agreed valuation methods rather than relying on dates alone.
  • Ignoring material ownership: Document quantities, location, payment records and ownership of materials funded by the homeowner.
  • Skipping inspections: Check work before it is concealed or followed by later stages, using qualified professionals when technical verification is required.
  • Approving undocumented changes: Record changes to scope, price and programme in writing before incorporating them into payment calculations.
  • Leaving handover conditions unclear: Specify final inspection, documentation, defects and retention release before work begins.
  • Delaying a payment that is due: Process payments within the agreed period once the contractual conditions and required documentation have been satisfied.

9. What Should a Construction Agreement Include?

Before signing, check that the agreement covers the following:

  • Scope and contract value: Drawings, BOQ, specifications, inclusions and exclusions.
  • Payment milestones: Amount, trigger, completion criteria and due date.
  • Advance terms: Purpose, adjustment and agreed safeguards.
  • Verification: Who inspects the work and what evidence is required.
  • Material procurement: Purchasing responsibilities, delivery and ownership.
  • Variations: Approval, pricing and schedule adjustments.
  • Taxes and documentation: Applicable taxes, invoices, receipts and deductions.
  • Delay and suspension: Procedures for delays, missed payments and justified extensions.
  • Retention and defects: Withheld amounts, rectification and release conditions.
  • Termination and disputes: Procedures for ending the contract and handling disagreements.
  • Handover: Applicable testing, manuals, warranties and completion documents.

For a large or complex project, ask an appropriate legal or construction professional to review the agreement before signing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a construction payment schedule?
It is a written plan connecting payments to agreed construction stages or milestones. It should specify the amount due, completion criteria and payment procedure.
What is the best payment schedule for house construction?
There is no universal best schedule. Choose one that reflects the actual scope, procurement responsibilities, measurable progress and agreed verification process.
Should I pay a contractor an advance?
An advance may be appropriate for mobilisation or specified procurement. Define its purpose, adjustment, documentation and appropriate safeguards in the contract.
Should construction payments be monthly or milestone-based?
Either method can work when clearly agreed. Milestone-based payments link amounts to defined progress; periodic payments may suit measured-work or valuation arrangements. The contract should explain how amounts are assessed.
What percentage should be retained until handover?
There is no universal percentage for every private residential contract. Agree on a proportionate amount, release conditions and deadline in writing.
What if a contractor requests payment before completing a milestone?
Review the agreement and request the agreed completion evidence. If a legitimate change is needed, document the revised terms rather than making an informal change.
How do I verify a construction milestone?
Compare completed work with the drawings, BOQ and contractual criteria. Maintain photographs, site records, invoices and inspection reports. Obtain qualified technical assessment where needed.
Are materials included in a turnkey payment schedule?
Only to the extent specified in the contract. Check the materials, brands or equivalents, allowances, exclusions and procurement terms.
Can a payment schedule change during construction?
The parties can agree to revised scope, quantities or commercial terms. Document the reason, revised amount and effect on the milestone sequence.
Should the final payment be made before defects are corrected?
Follow the agreement. Check handover conditions, outstanding defects and any agreed retention terms before releasing the corresponding amount.

Conclusion

A construction payment schedule connects financial planning with actual construction progress. Clear milestones, documented inspections, defined advance arrangements and written handover conditions help homeowners and contractors understand their responsibilities throughout a project.

Before construction begins, agree on the BOQ, scope, payment triggers, material ownership, variation process and retention terms. During construction, verify each stage and keep a record of payments and approvals.
